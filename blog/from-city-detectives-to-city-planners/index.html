From city detectives to city planners: The city through the eyes of children

The Urban Observations project demonstrates a systematic methodology for translating the ground-level urban experiences of children into actionable, structured data to assist urban planners and municipal decision-makers. Utilizing the City Layers citizen science platform and a specialized physical-digital toolkit, the project successfully mapped over 7,000 data points and 100 design proposals from children aged 8–18 across Vienna, Austria.

Core Framework & Project Intent

  • Children as Expert Citizens: The methodology is built on urban planning discourse that views children as "indicator species" for healthy, sustainable cities. If an urban environment is safe and accessible enough for a child to navigate autonomously, it inherently works for all demographics, including the elderly and disabled.

  • Rights and Competencies: The framework aligns with Article 12 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, which mandates children’s rights to express views on matters affecting them. Research proves children experience space through holistic, highly sensitive sensory dimensions, capturing micro-spatial variables that adults routinely overlook.

Two-Phase Development Timeline

The project progressed through distinct iterative cycles to refine its toolsets:

Phase 1: Exploration (2023)

This initial pilot translated abstract urban planning indices into a gamified, child-accessible mapping toolkit combining physical and digital formats. Testing during events like the OeAD Citizen Science Award 2023 helped pivot the framework away from rigid, scaled ratings and toward capturing direct sensory experiences and drawn solutions.

Phase 2: Implementation (Summer 2024)

Over June and July 2024, the team partnered with more than 50 school classes in Vienna, deploying three distinct mapping methodologies tailored to developmental stages:

  • Ages 8–12: Utilized the physical Neighborhood-Explorer Game worksheet to record spatial feelings, climate comfort, and drawings of physical proposals.

  • Ages 12–15: Used a hybrid model of simplified digital and physical workflows.

  • Ages 15–18: Fully deployed the mobile City Layers digital platform for geo-located documentation, photography, and text summaries.

Key Empirical Findings

The aggregation of 7,000+ spatial data points revealed six distinct patterns regarding how younger demographics perceive and navigate the built environment:

  • The "Langweilig" (Boring) Problem: The word "boring" appeared 147 times, almost exclusively linked to mono-functional, adult-designed spaces. Even aesthetically polished areas were rejected if they lacked interactive elements or opportunities for active engagement.

  • Human-Centric Negotiation: Children intuitively prioritize pedestrian spaces. Their design proposals frequently ignored vehicular infrastructure entirely, focusing instead on pedestrian "desire lines" and spontaneous paths suited to their scale.

  • Embodied Sensory Mapping: Submissions captured acute micro-climate and physical data, pinpointing specific surfaces dangerous for falling, wind tunnel corners, persistent puddles, and seating made unusable by extreme summer heat.

  • Civic Accountability: When evaluating highly littered spaces like Resselpark, children did not view cleanliness as a passive municipal service. Instead, they proposed systematic, collective community solutions, such as school cleanup rotations.

  • Infrastructure Priorities: Frequency analysis mapped out a clear data-driven manifesto for youth infrastructure demands:

    1. Skate, parkour, and active play equipment (6,575 mentions).

    2. Public drinking fountains (5,893 mentions).

    3. Trash and recycling bins (5,761 mentions).

    4. Flowers and natural greenery (5,741 mentions).

    5. Structural weather and climate protection (5,497 mentions).

  • Alternative Geographies: Spatial heatmaps revealed that children consistently value informal, unprogrammed social spaces (such as a specific wall perfect for ball games or modular structural blocks) over formally designated playground zones.

Methodological Breakthroughs & Future Outlook

The Archive of Experience: A critical methodological finding emerged when contrasting guided team sessions with autonomous neighborhood mapping. While guided field trips yielded general execution observations, independent mapping within a child's home neighborhood produced highly sophisticated spatial analysis (e.g., precise traffic light timing reviews). Meaningful citizen science requires structured training followed by independent exploration of familiar territories where daily spatial memory has accumulated.

Limitations and Scale Challenges

As a localized pilot program, the study noted several boundaries to address in future iterations:

  • Seasonal Context: Data collection was restricted to summer, missing variations like winter ice navigation or spring rainfall patterns.

  • Integration and Scale: Future development must find systematic pathways to integrate these localized citizen science data layers into formal municipal district planning mechanisms, and test if the methodology can scale up to address macro city systems like public transit networks and regional climate adaptation.
